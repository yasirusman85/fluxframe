# FluxFrame — narrated demo script

This is the shooting script for the FluxFrame walkthrough video. The goal is not to show that the screens exist — it is to explain *why each one is built the way it is*, and to be completely straight about which parts are real AI, which parts are real rendering, and which parts are simulated product surface. Every scene has three blocks: **Screen** (exactly what to click), **Say** (what to narrate, first person), and **Callouts** (what to point at, zoom into or highlight in the edit).

**Recording setup.** Chrome at **1440×900**, dark theme, browser zoom 100 %, storage cleared before the take (Application → Clear site data, or just run the tour below). Screen capture at 1080p/30. The underlying b-roll is reproducible: `npm run demo:record` drives the exact same sequence through Playwright (`e2e/demo/tour.spec.ts`) and writes `demo/walkthrough.webm`, so you can either narrate over a live screen recording or cut the narration over the Playwright capture. Use `DEMO_LIVE=1 npm run demo:record` if you want the real Pollinations endpoint (with its real ~15 s waits) in shot rather than the deterministic mock.

**Runtime.** As written the read-through is **8:50**. A walkthrough that only points at screens is worse than no walkthrough, so the budget is deliberately spent on *why*: do not cut below 7:00, and if you need to land under 8:00, trim the playback pauses in scene 4 and the graph animation in scene 7 rather than the explanations.

## [0:00] — 1. Intro & the problem

**Screen**
Start on the FluxFrame Explore page at `/` with the window already at 1440×900. Do not click anything for the first fifteen seconds; let the hero and the showcase montage sit on screen while you talk. At about 0:20, hover the "How it works" strip so the three cards — *Deterministic prompt enhancer*, *AI keyframe via Pollinations*, *In-browser render* — are on screen as you say what is real.

**Say**
"This is FluxFrame — a Higgsfield-style AI creative studio that runs entirely in the browser: no backend, no API keys, no sign-up. I want to start with the honest part, because it is also the interesting engineering part. The images you are about to see are real AI, generated by the public Pollinations endpoint. The video is real too — real `.webm` files you can download and open in any player — but it is rendered in your browser by a camera engine I wrote, not by a diffusion video model. And the credits, plans and API keys are simulated on purpose, so the whole product flow can be exercised without pretending I have a billing system. Everything I say in the next eight minutes is something you can verify from the repo, and the app itself labels the provider on every single output."

**Callouts**
- Zoom the "How it works" cards while narrating "three steps, no servers of our own".
- Lower-third text: **Real: Pollinations images · in-browser rendered video files. Simulated: credits, plans, API keys.**

## [1:15] — 3. Image Studio: real AI, real rate limits

**Screen**
In `/create/image`, clear the prompt and type: *"A red fox stepping onto a frozen lake at dawn, low winter sun, long blue shadows, breath in the air"*. Open the **Engine** dropdown and scroll through the six engines so the credit cost and "via Pollinations" line are visible; pick **Flux Realism v2**. Click **16:9**, then **High** quality. Press **⌘↵**. Narrate over the queue panel while it runs — especially any *"Public endpoint is rate-limited — waiting 12s for the next slot"* line. When the image lands, hover the **Pollinations** provider badge to show its tooltip.

**Say**
"This is a real request to a real endpoint, and that shapes the whole client. Pollinations is anonymous and rate-limited to roughly one request every fifteen seconds, so I serialise every request through a single promise chain — if three jobs are running, they queue politely instead of tripping over each other's quota. A 403 or 429 sets a shared back-off that every pending job respects, and the client retries up to three times, surfacing exactly what it is waiting for in the queue panel rather than showing a spinner and hoping. One more piece of honesty: the endpoint currently serves a single model, so I do not claim these six engines switch models — they attach different style suffixes to your prompt, and the badge on the output tells you where the pixels actually came from. If the endpoint is down entirely, you get a deterministic procedural render clearly labelled 'Procedural fallback', never a silent failure and never a fake."

**Callouts**
- Zoom the queue panel's stage text during the wait — this is the proof the rate limit is handled, not hidden.
- Freeze-frame on the **Pollinations** badge tooltip: "Real AI image from the public Pollinations endpoint".
- If you want the retry path on camera, run this scene with `DEMO_LIVE=1` twice in a row — the second request hits the 15 s window.

## [2:15] — 4. Cinema Studio: camera choreography to a real file

**Screen**
Go to `/create/cinema`. Type a prompt, then drag the keyframe file onto the **Keyframe** dropzone so the upload path is on camera. Click the **Hero Orbit** preset and let the small live preview loop run for three seconds. Drag the **Orbit** and **Dolly** sliders and show the preview responding. Set focal length **85 mm** and aperture **f/1.4**. Choose **3 s**, click **Generate**, and narrate over the "Rendering frame 42 / 90 → Encoding video…" stages. When the clip appears, press play and let it run the full three seconds, scrub it, then click **Download** and open the downloaded `.webm` in QuickTime or VLC — on camera.

**Say**
"Here is where I have to be most careful about what I claim. There is no video diffusion model behind this. What there is, is a camera engine: the keyframe is drawn to an off-screen canvas at thirty frames a second with pan, tilt, zoom, dolly, orbit and roll applied per frame, plus focal length driving the field of view and aperture driving the vignette — and that canvas is captured with MediaRecorder into an actual WebM file. The preview you just saw uses the same drawing code as the renderer, so what you preview is what you get. Two details I am proud of: frames are driven by wall-clock time rather than a frame counter, so a three-second clip is three seconds no matter how slow the draw is, and because Chrome's WebM output ships without a duration header, the blob is patched afterwards — otherwise scrubbing and the time display are broken. And the proof it is a real file is right here: it downloads, and it plays in a normal video player with nothing from FluxFrame involved."

**Callouts**
- Split-screen the live preview and the finished clip to show they match.
- Zoom the provider badge: **Motion engine** — say the words "not a diffusion model" while it is on screen.
- The money shot of the whole video: the `.webm` playing in QuickTime/VLC with the file size visible in Finder.

## [3:45] — 5. LipSync Studio: driven by the audio itself

**Screen**
Go to `/create/lipsync`. Upload a portrait to the **Portrait** dropzone. Drag the **Mouth X** and **Mouth Y** calibration sliders and show the pivot marker moving onto the mouth. Switch to the **Audio** mode tab and upload a short WAV or MP3. Set **Amplitude** to about 70. Generate, then play the result with the sound up so the mouth and the audio are obviously in sync.

**Say**
"LipSync takes a portrait and an audio file, and the mouth motion is computed from the audio, not faked on a timer. The file is decoded with the Web Audio API into a per-frame loudness envelope — normalised at the 95th percentile with a fast attack and a slower release, so it reads as speech rather than as a VU meter — and that envelope drives a jaw slice below the mouth point plus a low-passed head nod. The original audio is muxed into the recording, so the clip you download has sound. Two deliberately visible limitations: there is no face detection, so instead of guessing where the mouth is and getting it wrong, the UI asks you to place the pivot with two sliders. And script mode — where you type dialogue instead of uploading audio — produces a silent clip with burned-in captions, because browsers will not let you capture speech synthesis into a file. I would rather ship the honest version of that than a clip that pretends to have a voice."

**Callouts**
- Zoom the Mouth X / Mouth Y sliders while saying "no face detection — the UI asks instead of guessing".
- Turn the volume up visibly in the edit for the playback; the audio being *in the file* is the point.

## Recording checklist

**Before you hit record**

- [ ] Window at **1440×900**, browser zoom 100 %, bookmarks bar hidden, one tab only, no extensions visible.
- [ ] **Dark theme** (the app ships dark; the Playwright demo project forces `colorScheme: "dark"`).
- [ ] **Storage cleared** — Application → Clear site data, or run the tour, which calls `resetStorage`. A library full of old junk is the fastest way to look unfinished.
- [ ] macOS **Do Not Disturb** on; Slack, Mail and Calendar quit. No notification may appear in an eight-minute take.
- [ ] Desktop cleaned and dock hidden if the Finder/QuickTime shot in scene 4 shows the desktop.
- [ ] Credits topped up (or freshly reset to the 1,000 starting balance) so no scene stalls on a top-up modal you did not plan.
- [ ] Decide mocked vs. live: default is fine for pacing; `DEMO_LIVE=1` if you want the real 15 s wait on camera.

**Audio**

- [ ] Mic levels peaking around −12 dB, no clipping; record a ten-second test and listen back before the real take.
- [ ] Room quiet; laptop fans settled (run one render before recording so the machine is warm and the fans are steady).
- [ ] Record narration on its own track so a fluffed sentence can be re-read without re-shooting the screen.

**B-roll and stills**

- [ ] `npm run demo:record` → `demo/walkthrough.webm` (1440×900, the same sequence as this script).
- [ ] `npm run demo:screenshots` → refreshes every PNG in `docs/screenshots/` used by the README.

## Q&A prep

Eight questions a reviewer is likely to ask, with the answers to have ready.

**1. Why not a real video diffusion model?**
There is no free, anonymous, browser-callable video endpoint, and I was not going to ask a reviewer for an API key or ship a key in a static bundle. So I built the thing I could make genuinely good offline — deterministic camera choreography over an AI keyframe — and labelled it precisely. The pipeline contract is the point: `motion` implements `Pipeline(ctx) → PipelineOutput`, so swapping in a hosted video model is one module, not a rewrite.

**2. Isn't calling it a "video studio" misleading, then?**
That is exactly why the provider badge exists on every output and says "Motion engine", why the README leads with a real-vs-simulated table, and why I say it out loud in the first thirty seconds of the demo. The output is a real video file — it just comes from camera choreography rather than generative frames, and the product never implies otherwise.

**3. How would you add a backend?**
Three seams are already cut for it. Pipelines are lazy modules behind one contract, so a server-side generation call replaces a renderer without touching a page. The asset store is id-addressed, so blobs can sync to object storage with the ids unchanged. And credits are a store with `spend`/`refund`/`grant` — that becomes an API client, and the runner's refund-on-failure path stays as it is. I would put the queue server-side first, because that is the one thing the browser genuinely cannot do: survive a reload.

**4. What happens when the endpoint is down or rate-limits you?**
Rate limit: a 403 or 429 sets a shared back-off that every queued job respects, the client retries up to three times, and the queue panel tells you how many seconds it is waiting. Hard failure: the image pipeline falls back to a deterministic procedural render labelled "Procedural fallback", the job still completes and the badge tells the truth. A pipeline that throws for any other reason marks the job failed, refunds the credits and offers a retry. Aborts are handled separately — a user cancel never refunds twice and never falls back.

**5. How do you test video in CI?**
The video studios render for real in headless Chromium — the Playwright project uses the full Chromium channel with `--autoplay-policy=no-user-gesture-required`, so `captureStream` and `MediaRecorder` behave like headed Chrome. The specs upload a fixture keyframe, choose the 3 s duration and then assert on the resulting element: that it is a `<video>` with a blob source, `readyState ≥ 2`, and a duration close to the requested length. That last assertion is what caught the missing WebM duration header. Pollinations is mocked to a fixture JPEG so the suite is fast, offline and deterministic, with one spec deliberately returning a 403 to exercise the back-off.

**6. Why are credits and plans simulated rather than left out?**
Because the interesting engineering is in the flows they unlock: cost per engine, insufficient balance opening a top-up, an automatic refund when a pipeline fails, a retry that re-charges, and a ledger where every entry links to its project. Leaving them out would have removed the failure paths I most wanted to get right. They are local, labelled and never billed — and the API keys are generated with `crypto.getRandomValues` and never leave the browser.

**7. What was the hardest bug?**
Recorded clips had the right frames but no duration — scrubbing was broken and the time display read `Infinity`. Two causes: Chrome's WebM output carries no duration header (fixed by patching the blob with `fix-webm-duration`), and the frame loop was driven by `requestAnimationFrame`, which throttles in background tabs and headless runs, so a 3 s clip could come out as 5 s of wall-clock. Driving frames from wall-clock time with `setTimeout` fixed the timing and made the headless E2E assertions stable.

**8. How much of this was AI-assisted, and how do you know it is correct?**
It was built with AI-assisted development, and `CAPTURE-TEST.md` says so plainly, including the fact that automatic prompt capture was not configured before development began — so nothing in the repo is presented as a captured transcript. What backs the code is the automated checks: strict TypeScript, ESLint with `any` banned, Vitest over the libraries and stores, a Playwright suite over the real product including video, mobile and accessibility, and a CI job that runs lint, typecheck, unit, build and E2E on every push. The demo you just watched is itself reproducible from `e2e/demo/tour.spec.ts`.
